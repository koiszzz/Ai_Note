# Victory! Supreme Court Says Constitution Protects People’s Location Data

> 数据源：EFF 数字权利动态
> RSS：[打开源文件](https://www.eff.org/rss/updates.xml)

> 作者：Andrew Crocker　|　发布时间：Mon, 29 Jun 2026 17:25:22 +0000　|　分类：Locational Privacy　|　原文：[打开链接](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/06/victory-supreme-court-says-constitution-protects-peoples-location-data)

------

<div class="field field--name-body field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden"><div class="field__items"><div class="field__item even"><p><span data-contrast="auto">You have an expectation of privacy in location data that reveals your movements in the physical world, and even short-term surveillance of these movements is a search subject to the Fourth Amendment, the U.S. Supreme Court </span><a href="https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-112_0am4.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="auto">ruled today in </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">Chatrie v. United States</span></i></a><i><span data-contrast="auto">. </span></i><span data-ccp-props="{}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">The case involved geofence warrants, a form of dragnet surveillance police have used to vacuum up location data from electronic devices of people who happen to be in the vicinity of a crime. EFF had joined the American Civil Liberties Union, the ACLU of Virginia, and the Center on Privacy &amp; Technology at Georgetown Law in </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/press/releases/eff-supreme-court-shut-down-unconstitutional-geofence-searches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">filing an amicus brief</span></a><span data-contrast="auto"> in the case.</span><span data-ccp-props="{}"> <br /></span></p>

<p class="take-action"><a href="https://supporters.eff.org/donate/chatrie--cd">JOIN EFF</a></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">The decision in </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">Chatrie </span></i><span data-contrast="auto">is important: It is the first digital surveillance decision by the Court since its landmark 2018 ruling </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">Carpenter v. United States, </span></i><span data-contrast="auto">which involved prolonged tracking of people’s movements using cell phone location data. The new case expands that ruling by confirming that even shorter-term surveillance of location data can constitute a search because it can still reveal “private matters,” including “a wealth of detail about a person’s familial, political, professional, religious, and sexual associations.” </span><span data-ccp-props="{}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">The case is also important because the Court also recognized the records generated by the apps on a user’s phone—records we necessarily share with third-party tech company—are a user’s “own” and require Fourth Amendment protection. This is true, regardless of whether those records are “emails, documents, photographs, [ ] calendars” or location data. This will likely have broad implications for data generated by other apps on our phones, even if we click “agree” to sharing that data with third-party tech companies. </span><span data-ccp-props="{}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">Geofence warrants don’t name a suspect or a specific individual or device the way typical warrants do. Instead, they compel companies—almost always Google—to provide information on every electronic device in a given area during a given time period. This creates a high risk of suspicion falling on innocent people and can reveal sensitive and private information about where individuals have traveled in the past.</span><span data-ccp-props="{}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">Geofence warrants are the digital equivalent of police going person to person, home to home, without suspicion that any device holder has a connection to a crime. This turns innocent bystanders into suspects, just for being in the wrong place at the wrong time. </span><span data-ccp-props="240}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">In </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">Chatrie</span></i><span data-contrast="auto">, a 2019 geofence warrant compelled Google to search the accounts of all its hundreds of millions of users to see if any one of them was within a radius police drew around a Northern Virginia crime scene. This area amounted to several football fields in size and encompassed numerous homes, businesses, and a church.</span><span data-ccp-props="{}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">A federal district court in Virginia in 2022 held that the geofence warrant plainly violated  the Fourth Amendment. If the police want to get information on every device in the area, they must also establish probable cause to search every person in the area, the court said. The judge noted the government lacked particularized probable cause as to every individual within the geofence, which swept up innocent people and covered over 70,000 square meters in a busy area.</span><span data-ccp-props="{}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">The decision set an important precedent in finding the warrant overbroad and unconstitutional and was later followed by a 2024 federal Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals ruling holding that </span><span data-contrast="none">geofence warrants</span><span data-contrast="auto"> are “categorically prohibited by the Fourth Amendment.” However, the Chatrie lower court allowed the government to use the evidence it obtained because it relied on the warrant in “good faith.” A much divided en banc panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit in 2025 affirmed this “good faith” finding in the lower court’s </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2022/03/federal-court-virginia-holds-geofence-warrant-violates-constitution" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">opinion</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">.</span><span data-ccp-props="240}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">Google</span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2023/12/end-geofence-warrants" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none"> in 2023 announced changes</span></a><span data-contrast="auto"> to how it stores location data, with the effect of eventually making it impossible for the company to respond to geofence warrants. Since July 2025, mass geofence searches of Google users’ location data have not been possible. </span><span data-ccp-props="240}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">However, Google is not the only company collecting location data, nor the only way for police to access mass amounts of data on people with no connection to a crime. As we’ve written about extensively, </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/press/releases/data-broker-helps-police-see-everywhere-youve-been-click-mouse-eff-investigation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">data brokers</span></a><span data-contrast="auto"> collect and aggregate location data from many different apps on our phones and provide that data to police. And police can use “</span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2022/05/massachusetts-highest-court-upholds-cell-tower-dump-warrant" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">cell tower dump</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">” warrants to get access to data on everyone within range of specific cell towers. Suspicionless searches like these drag a net through vast swaths of information in hopes of identifying previously unknown suspects—ensnaring innocent bystanders along the way.</span><span data-ccp-props="240}"> </span></p>

<p><i><span data-contrast="auto">Chatrie</span></i><span data-contrast="auto"> could have wide-ranging implications beyond location data as well. The Supreme Court affirmed that app data is subject to the Fourth Amendment, because users “reasonably view” it as their own and reasonably expect it “to be shielded from the ‘inquisitive eyes’ of the government.” Justice Gorsuch, in an opinion concurring in the judgment, called location data a user’s “personal property,” no different from myriad other “effects” explicitly protected by the text of the Fourth Amendment.  As the Court concluded, “the point of carrying smartphones is to use what is on them,” so the Fourth Amendment has to protect more than just location data generated by the act of carrying the phone itself.</span><span data-ccp-props="240}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">The Court ultimately did not decide whether the particular warrant at issue in </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">Chatrie</span></i><span data-contrast="auto"> was “reasonable” or whether the “good faith” doctrine applied. The case now heads back to the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals to address these questions. </span><span data-ccp-props="240}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">But regardless of how the Fourth Circuit rules on remand, this </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">Chatrie</span></i><span data-contrast="auto"> opinion will shape how lower courts address police access to location and other data going forward. We look forward to citing </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">Chatrie</span></i><span data-contrast="auto"> to press future courts to recognize broad Fourth Amendment protections for user data.</span></p>
