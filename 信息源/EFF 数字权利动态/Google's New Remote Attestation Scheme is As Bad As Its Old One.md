# Google's New Remote Attestation Scheme is As Bad As Its Old One

> 数据源：EFF 数字权利动态
> RSS：[打开源文件](https://www.eff.org/rss/updates.xml)

> 作者：Cory Doctorow　|　发布时间：Thu, 09 Jul 2026 09:15:49 +0000　|　分类：Competition　|　原文：[打开链接](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/07/googles-new-remote-attestation-scheme-every-bit-terrible-its-old-remote)

------

<div class="field field--name-body field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden"><div class="field__items"><div class="field__item even"><p><span data-contrast="auto">Google owes its existence to the open web, but today, its technological “innovations” have much to do with locking users into a “walled garden.” The latest of these is “</span><a href="https://support.google.com/recaptcha/answer/16609652"><span data-contrast="none">reCAPTCHA Mobile Verification</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">,” an experimental initiative that will let companies block users if they are running independent, "de-googled" versions of Android. These “indie Android” versions are favored by people who want to protect their privacy and their attention by blocking trackers and ads. Worse, this is just the latest in a line of similarly user-hostile measures.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">Long before “agentic AI,” we had the idea that software would act as your agent on the internet. That's why the old-fashioned technical term for a browser is a “user agent.” Your browser acts on your behalf to retrieve information and then show it to you, in the format you choose. </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2013/10/lowering-your-standards"><span data-contrast="none">It's your agent</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">This is a powerful and profound idea. It is because browsers are our “agents” that we expect them to accept our directives, say, by blocking pop-ups, or by turning off autoplay sound, or by </span><a href="https://privacybadger.org/"><span data-contrast="none">blocking commercial surveillance trackers</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">Your browser does all that because your browser works for </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">you</span></i><span data-contrast="auto">. The reason your browser </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">can</span></i><span data-contrast="auto"> work for you is that the web is an open, standardized technology. In theory, anyone who follows the standards published by the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) can make a browser, and that web browser can connect to </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">any</span></i><span data-contrast="auto"> web server. Browsers and servers are </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">interoperable</span></i><span data-contrast="auto">. It's the same force that means you can put anyone's gas in your gas-tank, or anyone's shoelaces in your shoes, or anyone's milk on your cereal.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">But what if manufacturers could dictate those choices to you? What if your light socket refused to use a lightbulb unless it was officially blessed by the socket's manufacturer? What if your dishwasher refused to wash your dishes unless you bought them from one of the manufacturer's “dish partners?” </span><a href="https://arstechnica.com/gaming/2020/01/unauthorized-bread-a-near-future-tale-of-refugees-and-sinister-iot-appliances/"><span data-contrast="none">What if your toaster refused to toast “unauthorized bread?”</span></a><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">It's hard to see how a company could win its market with this strategy. After all, if the dishes are really better than the competition's, you'd buy them voluntarily, without any need for law or technology to force the matter. The only reason to make a dishwasher that refuses a rival's dishes is if the manufacturer's own dishes are ugly, expensive, and/or badly made.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">But once a company owns the market<span>—</span>once they've achieved dominance by buying out their rivals; by bribing potential competitors to stay out of their lane; and by engaging in deceptive conduct to trap key suppliers and customers<span>—</span>they can cement their dominance by blocking interoperability, keeping out rival dishes, milk, gas, lightbulbs, shoelaces and bread, capturing their whole market and </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">squeezing</span></i><span data-contrast="auto"> it.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p class="pull-quote"><span data-contrast="auto">Once a company owns the market, they can cement their dominance by blocking interoperability.</span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">That's what Google has done, and that's what Google wants to do more of Google's commercial behavior has been so unethical, deceptive and abusive that the company </span><a href="https://www.bigtechontrial.com/p/google-loses-the-adtech-monopolization"><span data-contrast="none">just lost </span><i><span data-contrast="none">three</span></i><span data-contrast="none"> federal antitrust cases</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">. This thrice-convicted monopolist </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/02/how-do-you-solve-problem-google-search-courts-must-enable-competition-while"><span data-contrast="none">paid Apple</span></a><span data-contrast="none"><span>—</span></span><span data-contrast="auto">more than $20b/year<span>—</span> to stay out of the search market: It </span><a href="https://www.thebignewsletter.com/p/boom-google-loses-antitrust-case"><span data-contrast="none">cheated app vendors</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">, ripping them off with sky-high junk fees and onerous conditions that raised prices while lowering the share of your spending that went to the companies whose products you were paying for. It</span><a href="https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/department-justice-prevails-landmark-antitrust-case-against-google"><span data-contrast="none"> cheated advertisers</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">, rigging the ad market to gouge businesses on ad prices and underinvesting to fight rampant ad-fraud, sucking hundreds of billions out of the productive economy for overpriced ads that no one saw.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">Google wasn't always this way. The “don't be evil” company owes its very existence to the open web ecosystem. When the company started to index the web in 1998, it was playing on an open field, where any web server could talk to any “user agent,” even one whose user was a startup like Google, that was making a copy of every page on the server.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">For years, Google thrived on the open web, and built open technologies. Android<span>—</span>the mobile operating system that Google bought in 2005 <span>—</span>was presented as an “open” alternative to existing mobile offerings, and as the mobile market collapsed into two companies<span>—</span>Google and Apple<span>—</span>Google always presented Android as the open alternative to Apple's “walled garden.” But there were always ways in which Google's “open” Android wasn't </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">exactly</span></i><span data-contrast="auto"> open. </span><a href="https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_18_4581"><span data-contrast="none">The company engaged in illegal “tying” arrangements</span></a><span data-contrast="auto"> that forced hardware vendors and carriers to lock out versions of Android that were created by Google's competitors.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">In other words, even though Google offered a mobile platform that was (mostly) </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">technically</span></i><span data-contrast="auto"> open, it found other ways to try to choke off the market oxygen for alternative Android versions that tried to capitalize on that technical openness.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">But life finds a way. The existence of an open, modifiable, tinkerer-friendly mobile operating system meant Android hackers could create alternatives to Google's (de facto) walled garden, which thrived in the cracks in that garden wall. Operating systems like </span><a href="https://calyxos.org/"><span data-contrast="none">CalyxOS</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">, </span><a href="https://pureos.net/"><span data-contrast="none">PureOS</span></a><span data-contrast="auto"> and </span><a href="https://grapheneos.org/"><span data-contrast="none">Graphene</span></a><span data-contrast="auto"> offered a more private, more secure Android experience, one that was largely “de-Googled,” blocking Google's relentless acquisition of your private data.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">And Google's data-hunger is </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">relentless</span></i><span data-contrast="auto">. Android exfiltrates a chunk of your personal and behavioral data </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">every five minutes</span></i><span data-contrast="auto">. </span><a href="https://digitalcontentnext.org/blog/2018/08/21/google-data-collection-research/"><span data-contrast="none">The “resting heartbeat” of Android surveillance pulses and pulses</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">, irrespective of whether you're using your device, and the instant you unlock your screen, that heartbeat quickens, sending even more data to the company. All that data has proven irresistible to authoritarian governments. Donald Trump's enforcers have seized on Google data as a vital source of information about the </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/04/google-broke-its-promise-me-now-ice-has-my-data"><span data-contrast="none">identity of protesters and the location of migrants hunted by ICE</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">So there are plenty of reasons why users would seek out these de-Googled alternatives to Android, finding them in spite of Google's efforts to block access to competing technologies. The worse it got, the better those alternatives looked.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">Perhaps this explains Google's years-long effort to increase the </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">technical</span></i><span data-contrast="auto"> barriers to using modified versions of Android, beefing these up to match the commercial restrictions that stand in the way of a de-Googled existence.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">Back in 2023, </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2023/08/your-computer-should-say-what-you-tell-it-say-1"><span data-contrast="none">Google floated the idea of “Web Environment Integrity” (WEI)</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">, a set of modifications to web standards that would force your computer to disclose its operating environment to the web servers it connected to, </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">even if you objected to this disclosure.</span></i><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">WEI was a form of “remote attestation.” That's when your device uses a sub-processor (sometimes called a “Technical Protection Module” or “TPM”) or a walled off part of its main processor (sometimes called a “secure enclave”) to produce a cryptographically signed description of your device and its configuration: which hardware, software, plug-ins, and settings you're running.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p class="pull-quote"><span data-contrast="auto">Take away our ability to block obnoxious digital content and you <i>guarantee</i> that we will be flooded with it.</span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">When you connect to a server, it demands that your device send this “attestation” before it handles your request. If your device won't provide this data, or if the server doesn't like (or recognize) your device and its details, it can refuse to deal with you. And because the attestation is prepared by a TPM or a secure enclave that you can't modify or override, you don't get to decide which facts about your device it's allowed to see.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">Practically speaking, this means that remote attestation lets a server refuse to deal with you until you turn off your ad-blocker and your tracker-blocker. It means that the server can discriminate against users who block auto-play sound and video, who block pop-ups, who put the tab in the background when it's playing a mandatory pre-roll ad.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">WEI was especially disturbing in light of Google's plan to kill ad-blockers and privacy blockers through updates to Chrome, </span><a href="https://protonprivacy.substack.com/p/google-is-finally-killing-ublock"><span data-contrast="none">an effort that continues to this day</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">These blockers are an important part of the dynamic between web publishers and their users. In the real world, when you get an offer, you can make a </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">counter-offer</span></i><span data-contrast="auto">. That's all an ad-blocker is: a way for users to respond to a server whose opening bid is, “How about you give me all your data and let me take over your computer in exchange for showing you this page?” with </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2019/07/adblocking-how-about-nah"><span data-contrast="none">“How about 'Nah?'</span></a><span data-contrast="auto">”</span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">We didn't get rid of pop-up ads by making them illegal, or by boycotting advertisers who used them. We got rid of pop-up ads when web users installed pop-up blockers, which made pop-up ads pointless. Take away our ability to block obnoxious digital content and you </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">guarantee</span></i><span data-contrast="auto"> that we will be flooded with it.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">These kinds of modifications aren't just used to block ads<span>—</span>they're also key to accessibility. People who have photosensitive epilepsy or suffer from low-contrast vision problems use add-ons to reformat pages so they can safely and legibly access them.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">WEI's creators said they were only trying to put the web on a level playing field with apps, which routinely disclose facts about your device to the companies whose servers you connect to, without asking you, and even if you don’t want them to. Apps are a source of bottomless enshittification, not least because (unlike the web), they enjoy special, dangerous legal protections that make it </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">very</span></i><span data-contrast="auto"> legally risky to modify them. WEI wasn't an effort to level the playing field between apps and the web<span>—</span>it was </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">a race to the bottom</span></i><span data-contrast="auto">, an attempt to make the web as enshittification-friendly as apps.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">Public outrage to WEI killed the project, but Google's commitment to augmenting its illegal commercial lockdown efforts with </span><i><span data-contrast="auto">technical</span></i><span data-contrast="auto"> lockdowns never ended. Now, </span><a href="https://support.google.com/recaptcha/answer/16609652"><span data-contrast="none">Google has rolled out an experimental “reCAPTCHA Mobile Verification”</span></a><span data-contrast="auto"> that uses an app, your camera, and your device's TPM or secure enclave to produce an attestation about your Android device.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><a href="https://www.reddit.com/r/PrivacySecurityOSINT/comments/1tbdjbj/privacy_concerns_around_googles_recaptcha_mobile/"><span data-contrast="none">This will make it much easier</span></a><span data-contrast="auto"> for the apps and other services you interact with to block your device if you run an Android alternative, or if you install a mod that overrides the actions of Google's stock Android.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="auto">This is a terrible idea<span>—</span>it's every bit as bad as WEI was. In an age in which Big Tech is ever-more tied to authoritarian governments, redesigning our devices to tell strangers things we don't want them to know isn't just shortsighted, it's inexcusable.</span><span data-ccp-props="276}"> </span></p>
