# Automated Moderation Is Here to Stay—Accountability Must Keep Pace

<p>When whistleblower Frances Haugen leaked a set of documents from Meta in 2020, among the revelations was a jarring statistic: The company’s algorithms designed to detect terrorist content incorrectly <a href="https://www.politico.eu/article/facebook-content-moderation-posts-wars-afghanistan-middle-east-arabic/">deleted nonviolent Arabic-language content</a> 77 percent of the time, while <a href="https://www.isdglobal.org/media-mentions/the-facebook-papers-hate-speech-filters-through-in-middle-east-thanks-to-lack-of-arabic-language-monitoring-and-faulty-ai-tech/">failing to detect hate speech</a> under the company’s own policies in many instances. Meta’s own transparency report released later that year <a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2020/10/facebooks-most-recent-transparency-report-demonstrates-pitfalls-automated-content">demonstrated similar findings</a>. Five years later, researchers in the region report that <a href="https://7amleh.org/post/meta-suppression-of-palestinian-content-en">overzealous moderation remains a problem</a>, while paths to remedy have all but collapsed.</p>

<p>Where these systems are faltering in Arabic, they’re positively failing in less-resourced languages. As a <a href="https://cdt.org/insights/content-moderation-in-the-global-south-a-comparative-study-of-four-low-resource-languages/">2025 report</a> from the Center for Democracy and Technology found, labeled datasets in certain languages and dialects such as Maghrebi Arabic and Kiswahili contain inconsistencies, bias, and inaccuracies due to the limited hiring of annotators who actually speak the languages as well as shifts in the languages themselves. An <a href="https://restofworld.org/2023/chatgpt-problems-global-language-testing/">investigation</a> into ChatGPT’s outputs in several low-resource languages demonstrates the depth of problem.</p>

<p>But language disparities are just one of several concerns as automated moderation becomes more widespread. From the <a href="https://www.hrw.org/report/2023/12/21/metas-broken-promises/systemic-censorship-palestine-content-instagram-and">systemic suppression of content from Palestine</a> to the repeated <a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2021/08/how-lgbtq-content-censored-under-guise-sexually-explicit">misclassification of LGBTQ+ content as adult or explicit material</a>, these varied examples demonstrate the risks of overreliance on automated moderation—and the need for stronger safeguards.</p>

<h3>Transparency, Cultural Competence, Appeals</h3>

<p>As we discussed in <a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/07/part-1-automated-moderation-here-stay">Part 1</a> of this series, automated systems can process content at a scale that humans never could, potentially enabling better moderation at scale and alleviating the psychological load on ill-paid moderators whose jobs require them to view incredibly disturbing content. But automated systems also reproduce existing biases, struggle to understand context, and often make mistakes that disproportionately affect journalists, activists, artists, and other vulnerable and marginalized communities.</p>

<p>As Rachel Griffin <a href="https://www.cigionline.org/articles/algorithmic-content-moderation-brings-new-opportunities-and-risks/">wrote in 2023</a>, “Perfectly accurate moderation is not only technically out of reach but intrinsically impossible.” Despite those intrinsic flaws, there is a great deal companies, policymakers, and civil society can do to help ensure that highly-automated systems operate in ways that respect human rights, minimize predictable harms, and provide meaningful accountability when they fail. If companies are going to continue relying on automation to moderate users’ speech—and there is little reason to believe they won’t—then accountability must evolve alongside these technologies.</p>

<p>That evolution can start with committing to the <a href="https://santaclaraprinciples.org/">Santa Clara Principles 2.0</a>. These principles, first outlined in 2020 and re-launched in 2021 after substantial international input, reflect the needs and expectations of the global community and specifically address automation. The first Foundational Principle states:</p>

<blockquote><p>Companies should ensure that human rights and due process considerations are integrated at all stages of the content moderation process, and should publish information outlining how this integration is made. Companies should only use automated processes to identify or remove content or suspend accounts, whether supplemented by human review or not, when there is sufficiently high confidence in the quality and accuracy of those processes. Companies should also provide users with clear and accessible methods of obtaining support in the event of content and account action. </p>

</blockquote>

<p>Drawing on the <span><a href="https://santaclaraprinciples.org/">Santa Clara Principles 2.0</a></span>, international human rights standards, and <a href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-forum-on-ai-law-and-governance/article/platforms-on-the-hook-eu-and-human-rights-requirements-for-human-involvement-in-content-moderation/63AB46C3687985F39187F923FA9F6341">years</a> of <span><a href="https://docs.un.org/en/A/73/348">research</a></span> documenting the shortcomings of automated moderation, we propose eight recommendations for policymakers thinking about regulation and companies deploying AI-assisted content moderation systems.</p>

<ol>

<li>Automated technologies should help, not replace, human moderators. For example, automated systems can help flag and prioritize content for review, while humans can interpret context, handle sensitive cases, and refine system performance.</li>

<li>Companies must be transparent about when and how automation is used in content decisions.</li>

<li>Companies must regularly audit their automated systems for bias, with particular attention to low-resource languages, vulnerable and marginalized communities, and conflict zones.</li>

<li>Users must have the ability to appeal, and to provide context when they believe human or automated moderation decisions have wrongfully removed their content. Appeals should be promptly evaluated and decided by human moderators.</li>

<li>Companies should regularly assess the human rights impact of their moderation decisions, and issue public statements of the results</li>

<li>If they rely on third-party vendors, companies should carefully (and regularly) audit those vendors for compliance with these same principles</li>

<li>Lawmakers should avoid promoting and passing legislation that effectively or explicitly mandates automated moderation systems</li>

<li>Policymakers should also refrain from attempting to dictate platforms technical and design choices to favor or disfavor particular expression.</li>

</ol>

<p>These recommendations understand that automated content moderation isn’t just a technical problem for clever engineers and product teams to solve. Because content moderation shapes public discourse and fundamental rights, its design and oversight must respond to the concerns of policymakers, civil society, independent researchers, and the communities most affected by these systems.</p>
