<h3><span data-contrast="none">Insecure Surveillance Cameras</span><span data-ccp-props="120}"> </span></h3>

<p><span data-ccp-props="0}">One of the signature gameplay elements of the Watch Dogs series is the ability for your character to hack into nearby security cameras from your phone and use that to gain a strategic advantage over hostile adversaries.</span><span data-ccp-props="0}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-ccp-props="0}">About a year before, that's exactly the issue that we were working on. EFF Technologist Cooper Quintin and I used the service </span><a href="https://www.shodan.io/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">Shodan</span></a><span data-contrast="none"> to </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2015/10/license-plate-readers-exposed-how-public-safety-agencies-responded-massive" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">identify a slew of automated license plate readers</span></a><span data-contrast="none"> (ALPRs) that Louisiana police had left unprotected on the internet. We found that the controls were open to anyone to manipulate and, just like in the game, you could watch the live video feeds.</span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="none"><img src="/files/2026/07/16/image4.png" width="657" height="562" alt="Shodan screencap of unprotected ALPR feeds" title="Shodan screencap of unprotected ALPR feeds" /></span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="none">We didn't use the data to acquire a skill point or collectible outfit. Instead, we forced police agencies to lock down their equipment and then used what we learned to persuade then Gov. Bobby Jindal to </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2015/10/license-plate-readers-exposed-how-public-safety-agencies-responded-massive" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">veto</span></a><span data-contrast="none"> a bill that would have created a new statewide surveillance dragnet. </span><span data-ccp-props="0}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="none">This issue still persists today. Most recently, security researchers </span><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uB0gr7Fh6lY&amp;t=1387s" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">Benn Jordan and Jon “GainSec” Gaines</span></a><span data-contrast="none">, and the </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/press/releases/electronic-frontier-foundation-present-annual-eff-awards-carolina-botero-connecting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">award-winning</span></a><span data-contrast="none"> journalists at </span><a href="https://www.404media.co/flock-exposed-its-ai-powered-cameras-to-the-internet-we-tracked-ourselves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">404 Media</span></a><span data-contrast="none">, uncovered how at least 60 pan-tilt-zoom cameras from the vendor Flock Safety were left exposed online.</span></p>

<h3><span data-contrast="none">Cell-Site Simulators</span><span data-ccp-props="120}"> </span></h3>

<p><span data-contrast="none">In Watch Dogs 2 there's a mission called "</span><a href="https://watchdogs.fandom.com/wiki/Stolen_Signals" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">Stolen Signals</span></a><span data-contrast="none">," in which Marcus and his best friend Wrench are trying to locate "stingrays," police devices that gather nearby cell-phone data by masquerading as legit cellular towers. We call these "</span><a href="https://sls.eff.org/technologies/cell-site-simulators-imsi-catchers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">cell-site simulators</span></a><span data-contrast="none">" (CSSs) and they're are an extremely alarming mass surveillance technology that allows police to track individual users through their phone identifiers. We've long advocated that this should require a search warrant. </span><span data-ccp-props="0}">  </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="none">Like Dedsec, we also had initiated a project to do the exact same thing. And in true Dedsec fashion, we also gave it a pop-culture name: </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2020/06/quick-and-dirty-guide-cell-phone-surveillance-protests" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">Crocodile Hunter</span></a><span data-contrast="none">, an homage to wildlife expert Steve Irwin, who had famously died after a stingray attack. </span><span data-ccp-props="0}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-ccp-props="0}">But while Marcus was running around Telegraph Hill, staff technologist Cooper Quintin and I were running around downtown San Francisco, testing out our own device for detecting suspicious cell phone towers during Salesforce's annual Dreamforce conference. And while we didn't find a CSS that day, we did find a mobile surveillance tower that a start-up had set up for the event.</span></p>

<p><span data-ccp-props="0}"><div class="caption caption-center"><div class="caption-width-container"><div class="caption-inner"><img src="/files/2026/07/16/image6.jpg" width="1059" height="794" alt="Cooper Quintin, EFF's own &quot;Wrench,&quot; testing out Crocodile Hunter at Dreamforce " title="Cooper Quintin, EFF's own &quot;Wrench,&quot; testing out Crocodile Hunter at Dreamforce " /><p class="caption-text">Cooper Quintin, EFF's own 'Wrench,' testing out Crocodile Hunter at Dreamforce</p></div></div></div></span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="none">Today, that project has evolved into </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/03/meet-rayhunter-new-open-source-tool-eff-detect-cellular-spying" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">Rayhunter</span></a><span data-contrast="none">, which allows anyone to use a cheap mobile hotspot to detect the type of cellular anomalies associated with CSSs. We're proud to say that now there's a whole international Dedsec-style network of researchers using this technology to look for surveillance </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/09/rayhunter-what-we-have-found-so-far" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">at protests, at the border, and in metropolitan areas</span></a><span data-contrast="none">. </span><span data-ccp-props="0}"> </span></p>

<h3><span data-ccp-props="0}">Security Robots</span><span data-ccp-props="120}"> </span></h3>

<p><span data-contrast="none">Throughout the game, Marcus encounters a number of autonomous pickle-shaped security robots wandering the city. At one point, Wrench reprograms one to become "Wrench Jr," a bona fide member of the Dedsec team. </span><span data-ccp-props="0}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-ccp-props="0}">In real life, these robots are made by a company called Knightscope, and EFF started shining light on them in </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2021/01/police-robots-are-not-selfie-opportunity-theyre-privacy-disaster-waiting-happen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">2020-2021</span></a><span data-contrast="none">, when they were first being deployed by companies and government agencies.</span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="none"><div class="caption caption-center"><div class="caption-width-container"><div class="caption-inner"><img src="/files/2026/07/16/image1.jpg" width="827" height="1103" alt="" title="" /><p class="caption-text">A Knightscope robot patrols a casino parking lot in Reno, Nevada. </p></div></div></div></span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="none">Today, law enforcement is </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/06/lawmakers-must-act-now-prevent-armed-police-drones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">pursuing</span></a><span data-contrast="none"> weaponized robots and drones, and EFF is at the forefront to stop this dystopian reality. In fact, in December 2022, we </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2022/12/victory-san-francisco-bans-killer-robotsfor-now" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">successfully fought</span></a><span data-contrast="none"> for San Francisco to ban the police department from weaponizing drones. In 2024, New York Police Department also </span><a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2024/02/02/nyregion/nypd-subway-robot-retires.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">retired</span></a><span data-contrast="none"> its subway robot.</span></p>

<h3><span data-contrast="none">A Citywide Surveillance "Operating System"</span><span data-ccp-props="120}"> </span></h3>

<p><span data-ccp-props="0}">In the Watch Dog series, one of the ominous developments is CTOS 2.0 (Central Operating System 2.0). Through this system, Blume, a government contractor, tries to collect a massive amount of data through citywide sensors and infrastructure, and to combine all that data into one unified<span>—</span>and totally insecure<span>—</span>analytics system. </span><span data-ccp-props="0}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-ccp-props="0}">At the time we'd only just begun to see this idea floated, with a limited number of cities trying tools like Palantir's Gotham to manage data.  </span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="none">Today, it is a frighteningly competitive market, particularly when it comes to law enforcement surveillance. For example, both Axon and Flock Safety are trying to offer products that integrate with every function of policing that sound like CTOs. In fact, Flock Safety product is literally titled, "Flock OS."</span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="none"><div class="caption caption-center"><div class="caption-width-container"><div class="caption-inner"><img src="/files/2026/07/16/image2.jpg" width="922" height="691" alt="" title="" /><p class="caption-text">Fusus demonstrated at a police chief's conference. </p></div></div></div></span></p>

<p><span data-contrast="none">Meanwhile, Axon's camera networking product, "</span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2023/05/neighborhood-watch-out-cops-are-incorporating-private-cameras-their-real-time" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">Fusus</span></a><span data-contrast="none">," sounds like it came straight from the Watch Dogs' writers room. Fusus allows for central live-streaming of all types of surveillance cameras in a city, including body-worn cameras, which was another prediction from the Watch Dog series that came true. </span><span data-ccp-props="0}"> </span></p>

<p><span data-ccp-props="0}">EFF has been part of many local battles to reject Flock and </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/03/guardrails-wont-protect-nashville-residents-against-ai-enabled-camera-networks" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">Axon</span></a><span data-contrast="none"> surveillance systems, and we've also </span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/06/dangers-consolidating-all-government-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><span data-contrast="none">advocated</span></a><span data-contrast="none"> against recent efforts at the federal level to consolidate government data. </span><span data-ccp-props="0}"> </span></p>
